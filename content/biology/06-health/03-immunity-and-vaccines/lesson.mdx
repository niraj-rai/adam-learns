<Hook emoji="🛡️">
Most people get chickenpox only once. After they recover, they can play with a friend who has it and not catch it again. Somehow, the body remembers. How? And can we give the body that memory without getting ill first?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="The second time your body meets the same germ, what happens?"
  options={["It reacts more slowly", "It reacts much faster and more strongly", "It can't react at all", "It reacts in exactly the same way"]}
  answer={1}
>
**Memory cells** recognise the germ at once, so antibodies are made much faster and in far greater amounts, usually before you even feel ill.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="The immune system" />

<ConceptCard title="Defenders" emoji="⚔️">
- **Barriers**: skin, mucus and stomach acid keep most germs out.
- **White blood cells** attack germs that get in. Some swallow germs; others make **antibodies**, proteins that lock onto one specific germ and help destroy it.
- After an infection, **memory cells** remain, ready to respond quickly next time. This is **immunity**.
</ConceptCard>

<Stage kind="try" title="Immune Memory" />

<Lab id="immune-memory" />

<ConceptCard title="Vaccines: a safe first meeting" emoji="💉">
A **vaccine** contains a weakened or killed germ, or a harmless part of it. It can't cause the full disease, but it trains the immune system and creates memory cells. India's Universal Immunisation Programme protects children against diseases like polio, measles, tetanus and tuberculosis.
</ConceptCard>

<DidYouKnow>
In 1796, Edward Jenner noticed that milkmaids who caught mild cowpox didn't get deadly smallpox. His cowpox inoculation became the first vaccine, and in 1980 smallpox became the first human disease to be eradicated worldwide.
</DidYouKnow>
