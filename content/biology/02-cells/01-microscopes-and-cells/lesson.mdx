<Hook emoji="🔬">
In 1665, the English scientist **Robert Hooke** looked at a thin slice of cork through a microscope he had built. He saw thousands of tiny boxes and called them **cells**, like the small rooms in a monastery. A few years later, the Dutch cloth merchant **Antonie van Leeuwenhoek** made even better lenses and saw tiny creatures swimming in pond water. A whole invisible world was waiting!
</Hook>

Higher magnification zooms in on a **smaller area**. The **field of view** at 400× is only a quarter as wide as at 100×, so always find your specimen on low power first!

<Stage kind="explain" title="Using a light microscope" />

<ConceptCard title="Parts and magnification" emoji="🔎">
- The **eyepiece** (usually 10×) is the lens you look through.
- The **objective lenses** (4×, 10×, 40×, sometimes 100× with oil) are near the slide.
- **Total magnification = eyepiece × objective**, e.g. 10 × 40 = **400×**.
- The **coarse and fine focus** knobs move the slide up and down until the image is sharp.
- **Stains** such as iodine (for plant cells) and methylene blue (for animal cells) colour the nucleus and other parts.
</ConceptCard>

<ConceptCard title="How big is a cell?" emoji="📏">
Cells are measured in <KeyTerm def="One millionth of a metre, or one thousandth of a millimetre. Symbol: µm.">micrometres (µm)</KeyTerm>: 1 mm = 1000 µm. If you know the width of the field of view, count how many cells fit across it:

**cell size ≈ field of view ÷ number of cells across**
</ConceptCard>

<DidYouKnow>
Leeuwenhoek's microscopes had a single tiny lens, but some could magnify about 270 times. He was the first person to see bacteria, and he kept his lens-making secrets to himself!
</DidYouKnow>
