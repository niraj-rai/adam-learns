<Hook emoji="🐸">
A frog lays thousands of tiny eggs in a pond. A hen sits on a few eggs for three weeks. A cow carries a single calf for about nine months. A Hydra simply grows a bud that breaks off! Why are there so many ways to make new animals?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="Why does a frog lay far more eggs than a hen?"
  options={["Frogs are bigger", "Frog eggs are fertilised outside the body in water and many are lost or eaten", "Hens don't need eggs", "It's random"]}
  answer={1}
>
Frog eggs are fertilised **outside** the body and left unprotected in water, where most are washed away or eaten. Laying lots increases the chance that some survive.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Sexual reproduction" />

<ConceptCard title="Two parents" emoji="🧬">
In **sexual reproduction**, a male **gamete** (sperm) fuses with a female **gamete** (egg). This is **fertilisation**, and it forms a single cell called a <KeyTerm def="The single cell formed when a sperm and an egg fuse. It divides repeatedly to form an embryo.">zygote</KeyTerm>, which divides again and again to become an **embryo**. The offspring get a mix of features from both parents, so they show **variation**.
</ConceptCard>

<Lab id="life-cycles" />

<ConceptCard title="Metamorphosis" emoji="🦋">
Some animals change their body form completely as they grow: a tadpole into a frog, or a caterpillar (larva) into a pupa and then an adult butterfly or silk moth. This is **metamorphosis**.
</ConceptCard>

- **Asexual reproduction**: budding in Hydra, binary fission in Amoeba.
