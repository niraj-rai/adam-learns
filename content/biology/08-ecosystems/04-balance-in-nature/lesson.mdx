<Hook emoji="🐅">
Imagine a forest where all the tigers are killed by poachers. At first, the deer seem lucky: nobody hunts them. But a few years later, the grass is gone, the soil is washing away and the deer are starving. How could losing tigers hurt the deer?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="What happens to deer numbers when their predators disappear?"
  options={["They stay the same", "They rise, overgraze, then crash", "They fall at once", "They become predators"]}
  answer={1}
>
Without predators, deer multiply beyond what the grass can feed. They **overgraze**, the food runs out, and many starve. Predators keep the system in **balance**.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Predators and prey" />

<ConceptCard title="Populations affect each other">
More prey means more food for predators, so predators increase. More predators means prey decrease. Then predators go hungry and decrease, and prey recover. In a healthy ecosystem, these ups and downs stay within limits.
</ConceptCard>

<RealLife>
**Project Tiger** began in India in 1973, when only about 1,800 tigers were left. Protected reserves, anti-poaching patrols and wildlife corridors have helped the population rise to more than 3,600 today, the most of any country.
</RealLife>

<DidYouKnow>
When wolves were brought back to Yellowstone Park in the USA in 1995, elk stopped overgrazing riverbanks. Trees regrew, beavers returned, and even the rivers changed course!
</DidYouKnow>
