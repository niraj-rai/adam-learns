<Hook emoji="🔥">
Make a row of squares from matchsticks. 1 square needs 4 sticks, 2 squares need 7, 3 squares need 10. How many for 100 squares? You could build it and count… or you could find a **rule** that works for any number of squares. That rule needs a number that can be *any* number: a **letter-number**.
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="Each new square adds 3 sticks. How many sticks for 10 squares?"
  options={["30", "31", "40", "33"]}
  answer={1}
>
The first square needs 4 sticks, and each of the 9 more adds 3: 4 + 27 = **31**. The rule 3n + 1 works for every n: 3 × 10 + 1 = 31.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Expressions" />

<ConceptCard title="Letter-numbers">
A <KeyTerm def="A letter that stands for a number that can change, like n or x">variable</KeyTerm> (letter-number) such as n can stand for any number. An <KeyTerm def="A combination of numbers, letters and operations, like 3n + 1">expression</KeyTerm> like 3n + 1 describes a whole pattern at once. We write 3n for 3 × n, and in 3n + 1 the 3 is a **coefficient** and the 1 is a **constant**.
</ConceptCard>

<DidYouKnow>
The Indian mathematician **Brahmagupta** (7th century CE) used short names of colours, like *kālaka* (black) and *nīlaka* (blue), as letters for unknown quantities, centuries before x and y became standard in Europe.
