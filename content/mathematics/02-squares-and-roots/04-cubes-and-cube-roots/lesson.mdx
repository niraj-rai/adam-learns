<Hook emoji="🚕">
In 1918, the English mathematician G. H. Hardy visited his friend **Srinivasa Ramanujan** in hospital. He mentioned that his taxi's number, 1729, seemed rather dull. Ramanujan replied instantly: *No, it is a very interesting number!* It is the smallest number that can be written as the sum of two cubes in two different ways. What are cubes, and how did he know?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="What is 4³ (“4 cubed”)?"
  options={["12", "64", "16", "43"]}
  answer={1}
>
4³ = 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. It's the number of small cubes in a 4 × 4 × 4 block. Cubing is **not** multiplying by 3.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Cubes and cube roots" />

<ConceptCard title="Cube numbers">
A <KeyTerm def="A number made by multiplying a whole number by itself three times, like 5 × 5 × 5 = 125">cube number</KeyTerm> is n³: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343, 512, 729, 1000, … The <KeyTerm def="The number which, cubed, gives n; written ∛n">cube root</KeyTerm> goes backwards: ∛125 = 5.
</ConceptCard>

<DidYouKnow>
1729 = 1³ + 12³ = 9³ + 10³. Numbers like this are now called **taxicab numbers** in honour of that conversation. The next one is 4104 = 2³ + 16³ = 9³ + 15³.
