<Hook emoji="🏔️">
On a winter morning in Leh, the thermometer reads −8 °C. The Dead Sea shore is about 430 m *below* sea level. A lift in a Bengaluru mall goes down to basement level B2. None of these can be described with only 0, 1, 2, 3… We need numbers on **the other side of zero**.
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="Which is colder: −7 °C or −2 °C?"
  options={["−7 °C", "−2 °C", "They are the same", "You can't compare them"]}
  answer={0}
>
−7 °C is 7 degrees below zero; −2 °C is only 2 below. On a number line, −7 is further **left**, so it is the **smaller** number, even though 7 is bigger than 2.
</PredictFirst>

<ConceptCard title="What are integers?">
<KeyTerm def="The whole numbers, their negatives, and zero: …, −3, −2, −1, 0, 1, 2, 3, …">Integers</KeyTerm> are all the whole numbers, their **opposites** (negatives) and zero. On a number line, numbers get bigger as you move right and smaller as you move left.
</ConceptCard>

<MythFact myth="Subtracting always makes a number smaller.">
Not with negatives! Subtracting −3 moves you 3 steps **right**. Think of money: if someone cancels a ₹3 debt you owe (takes away −3), you are ₹3 better off.
</MythFact>

<DidYouKnow>
Indian mathematician **Brahmagupta** wrote rules for zero and negative numbers (he called them “debts” and “fortunes”) in 628 CE, around a thousand years before most European mathematicians accepted them.
</DidYouKnow>
