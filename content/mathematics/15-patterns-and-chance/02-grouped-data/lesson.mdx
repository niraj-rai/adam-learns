<Hook emoji="📏">
Your school measures the heights of 400 students. A list of 400 numbers tells you almost nothing at a glance. Group them into classes like 140–145 cm, 145–150 cm, … and draw a histogram, and suddenly you can see the typical height and how spread out they are.
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="A class is 20–30. What value do we use to represent it when estimating the mean?"
  options={["20", "30", "10", "25"]}
  answer={3}
>
We use the **midpoint**, (20 + 30) ÷ 2 = 25, because we don't know the exact values inside the class.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Grouping data" />

<ConceptCard title="Histograms" emoji="📊">
For continuous data (heights, times, marks), group values into <KeyTerm def="A range of values used to group data, such as 150–155 cm. The class width is the size of the range.">class intervals</KeyTerm> of equal width and count the frequency of each. A <KeyTerm def="A chart for grouped continuous data where touching bars show the frequency of each class.">histogram</KeyTerm> shows them as touching bars.
</ConceptCard>

<Formula tex="\bar{x} \approx \frac{\sum f \times m}{\sum f}" caption="Estimated mean of grouped data: f = frequency, m = class midpoint" />

<ConceptCard title="Weighted averages" emoji="⚖️">
When some values count more than others, use a weighted mean: Σ(weight × value) ÷ Σweights. Your school's final grade might weight exams 60% and projects 40%. The grouped-data mean is a weighted mean where the weights are the frequencies.
</ConceptCard>
