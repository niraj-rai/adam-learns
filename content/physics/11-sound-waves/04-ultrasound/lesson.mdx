<Hook emoji="🦇">
A bat flying in total darkness can catch a mosquito. A doctor can watch a baby's heart beating before it is born. A ship can map a sea floor 4 km below. All three use sounds that are too high-pitched for us to hear: **ultrasound**.
</Hook>

<ConceptCard title="SONAR" emoji="🚢">
<KeyTerm def="SOund Navigation And Ranging: sending out ultrasound pulses and timing their echoes to find the distance, direction and speed of underwater objects.">SONAR</KeyTerm> sends ultrasound pulses down from a ship and times the echo from the sea bed or a shoal of fish:

depth = speed of sound in water × time ÷ 2

Ultrasound is used because its short wavelength gives sharp echoes and travels in a narrow beam without spreading much.
</ConceptCard>

<RealLife>
Ultrasound scans (sonography) send 1–15 MHz pulses into the body. Echoes from the boundaries between tissues build up a picture of organs or an unborn baby. Unlike X-rays, ultrasound doesn't damage cells, so it's safe for repeated scans. Engineers use it to find hidden cracks inside metal parts of bridges, trains and aircraft.
</RealLife>

<DidYouKnow>
Bats use <KeyTerm def="Finding objects by sending out sounds and listening for their echoes, as bats and dolphins do.">echolocation</KeyTerm>: they send out up to 200 ultrasonic clicks a second as they close in on an insect, timing each echo to track it precisely.
</DidYouKnow>
