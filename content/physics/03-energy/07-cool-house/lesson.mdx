<Hook emoji="🏡">
In a heatwave, air conditioners run day and night, which uses huge amounts of electricity and pumps even more heat into the streets. Yet old houses in Chettinad, Rajasthan and Kerala stayed comfortable for centuries without any electricity. Can you design a home that uses physics, not power?
</Hook>

<ConceptCard title="Your toolkit" emoji="🧰">
- **Conduction**: insulate walls and roofs with trapped air (rat-trap bond, clay tiles with air gaps).
- **Convection**: let breezes flow through (cross ventilation, courtyards) to carry hot air out.
- **Radiation**: reflect sunlight (white roofs, light paint) and block it (shade trees, sunshades).
- **Evaporation**: plants and water cool the air around them.
- In winter, you want the opposite: trap heat in and let the low winter sun shine in.
</ConceptCard>

<RealLife title="Laurie Baker's rat-trap bond">
The architect **Laurie Baker** built hundreds of low-cost, comfortable homes in Kerala. His favourite trick was the **rat-trap bond**: bricks laid on edge to leave air gaps inside the wall. The trapped air insulates, and the wall uses about a quarter fewer bricks!
</RealLife>

<Inquiry criterion="D" title="A cooler city">
Air conditioners cool rooms but make cities hotter outside. Suggest at least three ways your city could stay cooler with less air conditioning. Justify each idea using conduction, convection, radiation or evaporation.
</Inquiry>
