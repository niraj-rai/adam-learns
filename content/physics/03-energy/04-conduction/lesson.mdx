<Hook emoji="🥄">
Leave a steel spoon in a cup of hot chai and, after a while, the handle gets hot. A wooden spoon in the same chai stays cool. The chai never touched the handle, so how did the heat get there?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="Spoons of copper, steel and wood stand in hot chai. Whose handle gets hot first?"
  options={["Wood", "Steel", "Copper", "All at the same time"]}
  answer={2}
>
**Copper** is one of the best conductors of heat, so its handle heats up fastest. Steel is slower. Wood is an **insulator**: its handle hardly warms at all.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Heat passed hand to hand" />

<ConceptCard title="What is conduction?" emoji="🔗">
<KeyTerm def="The transfer of heat through a material, from particle to particle, without the material itself moving.">Conduction</KeyTerm> is how heat travels through solids. Particles at the hot end vibrate faster and bump into their neighbours, passing energy along the line, like a crowd passing a message. In metals, free-moving electrons carry energy along even faster.
</ConceptCard>

<MythFact myth="Metal objects are colder than wooden objects in the same room.">
Left in the same room, they're at the **same temperature**. Metal *feels* colder because it conducts heat away from your hand much faster.
</MythFact>

<DidYouKnow>
Many Indian kitchens use copper-bottomed steel pots. Steel is tough and doesn't react with food, while the copper base spreads heat evenly across the bottom so food doesn't burn in one spot.
</DidYouKnow>
