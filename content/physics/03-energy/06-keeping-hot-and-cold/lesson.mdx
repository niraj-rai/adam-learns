<Hook emoji="🧴">
Pour hot chai into a vacuum flask in the morning, and it's still hot at lunch. Pour ice-cold water into the same flask, and it stays cold. How does a flask "know" whether to keep things hot or cold?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="Chai at 90 °C is poured into four containers. Which will keep it above 60 °C for two hours?"
  options={["Steel tumbler", "Clay kulhad", "Thermocol cup", "Vacuum flask"]}
  answer={3}
>
Only the **vacuum flask**. It doesn't know hot from cold: it simply **slows down all heat flow**, in either direction.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Stopping heat flow" />

<ConceptCard title="How a vacuum flask works" emoji="🔬">
- The **vacuum** between the double glass or steel walls has no particles, so heat can't conduct or convect across it.
- The **silvered** surfaces reflect radiation back.
- The **stopper** stops evaporation and convection from the top.

The vacuum flask was invented by the Scottish scientist **James Dewar** in 1892 to keep chemicals very cold.
</ConceptCard>

<ConceptCard title="Trapped air: nature's insulator" emoji="🧣">
Air is a very poor conductor, and when it's **trapped** in small pockets it can't form convection currents either. That's why woollens, quilts (razais), thermocol, feathers and double-glazed windows keep things warm.
</ConceptCard>

<Lab id="flask-lab" />

<RealLife title="The clay matka: India's ancient fridge">
Water slowly seeps through tiny pores in a clay matka and **evaporates** from its outer surface. Evaporation needs heat, which it takes from the pot and the water inside, so the water stays cooler than the room. It works best in dry, hot weather.
</RealLife>

<ConceptCard title="Reading a cooling curve" emoji="📉">
A cooling curve starts **steep** (a hot drink loses heat fastest when it's much hotter than the room) and then **flattens** as it approaches room temperature. The better the insulation, the flatter the curve.
</ConceptCard>
