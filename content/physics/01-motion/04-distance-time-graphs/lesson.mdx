A flat line means the **distance isn't changing** as time passes: the object is **stopped**. It's one of the most common graph mistakes, so remember it!

<Stage kind="explain" title="Reading the graph" />

<ConceptCard title="Distance–time graphs" emoji="📊">
- **Time** goes along the bottom (x-axis); **distance** goes up the side (y-axis).
- A **straight sloping line** = moving at a **constant speed** (uniform motion).
- A **steeper** line = **faster**.
- A **flat** line = **stopped**.
- A line going **down** = moving **back** towards the start.
- A **curved** line = the speed is **changing**.
</ConceptCard>

<Formula tex="\text{speed} = \text{slope} = \frac{\text{change in distance}}{\text{change in time}}" />

<StepReveal>
<div>
**Worked example:** a graph goes from (0 s, 0 m) to (4 s, 12 m). What is the speed?
</div>
<div>
Change in distance = 12 − 0 = **12 m**
</div>
<div>
Change in time = 4 − 0 = **4 s**
</div>
<div>
Speed = 12 ÷ 4 = **3 m/s**, which is Adam running!
</div>
</StepReveal>

<MythFact myth="A distance–time graph is a picture of the route: a hill on the graph means going uphill.">
The graph shows **how distance changes with time**, not the shape of the road. A line going up steeply means **moving fast**, even on a flat road. A straight road and a winding road can have exactly the same graph!
</MythFact>

<DidYouKnow>
Railway engineers draw "time–distance" diagrams for every train on a line. Where two lines cross, two trains are at the same place at the same time, so the plan must make sure they're on different tracks. Indian Railways runs over 13,000 passenger trains a day!
</DidYouKnow>
