<Hook emoji="☀️">
At Jantar Mantar in Jaipur stands the **Samrat Yantra**, a giant sundial built around 1734, taller than a 7-storey building. It tells local time to within about **2 seconds** using only the Sun's shadow. How did people measure time before clocks and phones?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="A pendulum with a HEAVIER bob, same string length. Its swings will be…"
  options={["Faster", "Slower", "The same", "It stops swinging"]}
  answer={2}
>
Surprisingly, **the same**! The time for one swing depends on the **length** of the string, not the mass of the bob (and hardly on how far you pull it back). Galileo noticed this in the late 1500s, reportedly by timing a swinging lamp in Pisa Cathedral against his own pulse!
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Keeping time with periodic motion" />

<ConceptCard title="Nature's clocks" emoji="🌗">
Anything that repeats regularly can measure time:

- **Day** (Earth spinning once), **month** (Moon's phases), **year** (Earth orbiting the Sun).
- In ancient India, a **ghati (ghatika)** was a copper bowl with a tiny hole that sank in water in a fixed time (about 24 minutes). Sundials, sand-timers and candle clocks were also used.
- Modern clocks use pendulums, vibrating **quartz crystals**, and even **atoms** (atomic clocks are accurate to one second in millions of years).
</ConceptCard>

<ConceptCard title="The simple pendulum" emoji="🕰️">
A **simple pendulum** is a small heavy **bob** hanging from a string. One **oscillation** is one complete swing, from one side to the other and back again. The **time period** is the time taken for one oscillation.

Measure the time for **10 oscillations** and divide by 10. This reduces the error from your reaction time when starting and stopping the stopwatch.
</ConceptCard>

<MythFact myth="A heavier bob makes the pendulum swing faster.">
The mass of the bob makes **no difference** to the time period. A heavier bob is pulled down harder by gravity, but it is also harder to get moving, and the two effects cancel out. Only the **length** matters (and the strength of gravity).
</MythFact>

<DidYouKnow>
The SI unit of time is the **second (s)**. Today, one second is defined by counting **9,192,631,770** vibrations of a caesium atom. India's official time (IST) is maintained by atomic clocks at the **National Physical Laboratory (CSIR-NPL)** in New Delhi.
</DidYouKnow>
