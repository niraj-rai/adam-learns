<Hook emoji="🎒">
After two hours of homework you feel you've done a lot of work. A porter carrying heavy luggage along a railway platform certainly has. But in physics, **work** has a precise meaning, and surprisingly, the porter walking along a flat platform does no work on the luggage at all!
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="You hold a heavy bag perfectly still for five minutes. How much work do you do on the bag?"
  options={["A lot: my arms are tired", "Zero: the bag doesn't move", "It depends on how heavy the bag is", "Exactly the bag's weight"]}
  answer={1}
>
Work needs **movement in the direction of the force**. The bag doesn't move, so no work is done on it. (Your muscles still use energy, which is why you get tired.)
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Work = force × displacement" />

<Formula tex="W = F \times s" caption="W = work (J), F = force (N), s = displacement in the direction of the force (m)" />

<ConceptCard title="When is work done?" emoji="✅">
Work is done when a **force** makes an object **move** in the direction of the force.
- 1 <KeyTerm def="The SI unit of work and energy. 1 J is the work done when a force of 1 N moves an object 1 m in the direction of the force.">joule (J)</KeyTerm> = 1 N × 1 m.
- Doing work **transfers energy**: lifting a bag gives it potential energy; pushing a trolley gives it kinetic energy. Work done = energy transferred.
</ConceptCard>

<Lab id="work-meter" />

<MythFact myth="A satellite orbiting Earth needs gravity to do work on it all the time.">
In a circular orbit, gravity pulls towards Earth's centre, at right angles to the satellite's motion. So gravity does **no work**, and the satellite's speed stays the same.
</MythFact>
