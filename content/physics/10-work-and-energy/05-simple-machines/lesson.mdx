<Hook emoji="🌍">
"Give me a place to stand, and a lever long enough, and I will move the Earth." Archimedes said this over 2200 years ago. Levers, pulleys and ramps are **simple machines**: they let a small force move a big load. They're everywhere: scissors, bottle openers, wells, flagpoles and wheelchair ramps.
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="A ramp lets you push a heavy trolley up to a stage with less force than lifting it. What's the catch?"

  answer={1}

The ramp reduces the **force** but you push over a longer **distance**. Force × distance (the work) stays about the same.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="How machines help" />

<ConceptCard title="Load, effort and mechanical advantage" emoji="⚖️">
- **Load**: the force the machine overcomes (e.g. the weight lifted).
- **Effort**: the force you apply.
- <KeyTerm def="How many times a machine multiplies the effort: load ÷ effort. MA greater than 1 means the machine multiplies force.">Mechanical advantage</KeyTerm> MA = load ÷ effort.
</ConceptCard>

<ConceptCard title="No free lunch: efficiency" emoji="🔧">
A machine can reduce the force, but **never the work**. In a perfect machine work in = work out. Real machines waste some energy to friction, so

<KeyTerm def="The fraction of the input energy (work) that becomes useful output: useful work out ÷ work in × 100%. Always less than 100% in real machines.">efficiency</KeyTerm> = useful work out ÷ work in × 100%, which is always less than 100%.
</ConceptCard>

<Formula tex="\text{efficiency} = \frac{\text{useful work out}}{\text{work in}} \times 100\%" />

<DidYouKnow>
Hill roads zigzag up mountains in hairpin bends. Each bend is part of a very long, gentle inclined plane, so vehicles need a much smaller force to climb, at the cost of a much longer drive.
</DidYouKnow>
