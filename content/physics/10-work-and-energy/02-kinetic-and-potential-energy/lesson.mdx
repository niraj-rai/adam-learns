<Hook emoji="🚗">
Road-safety posters say that a small increase in speed makes crashes much more dangerous. That's not just a slogan: a car's kinetic energy grows with the **square** of its speed. Double the speed and there's four times as much energy to get rid of in a crash.
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="A scooter speeds up from 10 m/s to 20 m/s. Its kinetic energy becomes…"
  options={["twice as big", "the same", "half as big", "four times as big"]}
  answer={3}
>
KE = ½mv². Doubling v makes v² four times bigger, so the kinetic energy is **four times** bigger.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Two energy stores" />

<ConceptCard title="Kinetic energy" emoji="🏃">
Every moving object has <KeyTerm def="The energy an object has because it is moving. KE = ½mv².">kinetic energy</KeyTerm>. It comes from the work done to speed it up.
</ConceptCard>

<Formula tex="KE = \tfrac{1}{2} m v^2" caption="m = mass (kg), v = speed (m/s), KE in joules" />

<ConceptCard title="Potential energy" emoji="🏔️">
Lifting an object against gravity stores <KeyTerm def="The energy stored in an object because of its height above the ground (gravitational) or because it is stretched or squashed (elastic).">potential energy</KeyTerm>. The work done lifting it (weight × height) is stored, ready to be released when it falls. A stretched catapult or a wound-up spring stores **elastic** potential energy.
</ConceptCard>

<Formula tex="PE = m g h" caption="m = mass (kg), g = 9.8 N/kg, h = height raised (m)" />

<RealLife>
Hydroelectric dams like those on the Kaveri and Bhakra store water high up. As it falls through the turbines, its potential energy becomes kinetic energy and then electrical energy that lights up cities.
</RealLife>
