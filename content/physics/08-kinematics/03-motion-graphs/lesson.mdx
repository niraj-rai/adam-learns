<Hook emoji="📈">
In Grade 7 you read distance–time graphs. Now you'll add a second, more powerful graph: **velocity–time**. From a single v–t graph you can read the velocity at any moment, work out the acceleration from its **slope**, and find how far you went from the **area** underneath it. Engineers design metro timetables exactly this way.
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="A velocity–time graph is a flat horizontal line at 10 m/s. What does it mean?"
  options={["The object is at rest", "The object moves at a constant 10 m/s", "The object is accelerating at 10 m/s²", "The object is going uphill"]}
  answer={1}
>
Flat on a **v–t** graph means the velocity isn't changing: constant velocity, zero acceleration. (Flat on a **displacement–time** graph would mean at rest. Always read the axes!)
</PredictFirst>

<DidYouKnow>
The idea that the area under a velocity–time graph gives distance was worked out by medieval scholars at Merton College, Oxford, in the 1300s, over 200 years before Galileo tested it with rolling balls.
</DidYouKnow>
