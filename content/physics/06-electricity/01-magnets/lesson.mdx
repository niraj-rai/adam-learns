<Hook emoji="🧲">
Legend says a shepherd found that the iron nails in his shoes stuck to a strange black rock. That rock was **magnetite**, a natural magnet, or lodestone. Sailors later hung lodestones on threads and found they always pointed the same way: the first compasses! Why do magnets attract some things and not others, and why do they point north?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="Which of these will a magnet attract?"
  options={["An aluminium can", "A copper wire", "A steel paper clip", "A gold ring"]}
  answer={2}
>
Only the **steel paper clip**. Steel is mostly iron. Aluminium, copper and gold are metals, but they are **not magnetic**.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Magnetic materials and poles" />

<ConceptCard title="What magnets attract" emoji="📎">
Magnets attract **magnetic materials**: iron, nickel, cobalt and alloys containing them, such as steel. Most other materials, including most metals, are **non-magnetic**.
</ConceptCard>

<ConceptCard title="Poles" emoji="🔴🔵">
Every magnet has a **north pole (N)** and a **south pole (S)**. The magnetic force is strongest at the poles.
- **Like poles repel** (N–N, S–S).
- **Unlike poles attract** (N–S).
- Cut a magnet in half and you get two smaller magnets, each with its own N and S!
</ConceptCard>

<ConceptCard title="Magnetic fields" emoji="🧭">
The space around a magnet where its force acts is its <KeyTerm def="The region around a magnet where a magnetic force can be felt. Shown by field lines running from N to S.">magnetic field</KeyTerm>. Iron filings and compasses show the **field lines**: they run from the N pole round to the S pole and are closest together where the field is strongest.
</ConceptCard>

<MythFact myth="A compass points north because the North Pole attracts its north end.">
Unlike poles attract, so the Earth's magnetic pole in the Arctic must actually be a magnetic **south** pole! We call it "north" because that's where it is on the map.
</MythFact>
