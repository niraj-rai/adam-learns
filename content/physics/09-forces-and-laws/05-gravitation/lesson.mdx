<Hook emoji="🪶">
In 1971, astronaut David Scott stood on the Moon and dropped a hammer and a falcon feather at the same moment. They hit the ground **together**. On Earth the feather drifts down slowly, but that's air resistance, not gravity. Gravity pulls on everything with mass, from apples to the Moon.
</Hook>

The 10 kg ball is pulled 10 times harder, but it also has 10 times the mass (inertia). a = F ÷ m is the same: **g ≈ 9.8 m/s²** for both.

<ConceptCard title="Mass and weight" emoji="⚖️">
- <KeyTerm def="The amount of matter in an object, measured in kilograms. It is the same everywhere.">Mass</KeyTerm> is how much matter something has (kg). It is the **same everywhere**, and it's also a measure of inertia.
- **Weight** is the force of gravity on that mass (N): **W = mg**. It **changes** with g.
- On Earth g ≈ 9.8 N/kg; on the Moon about 1.6 N/kg. A 60 kg student weighs about 590 N on Earth but only about 96 N on the Moon.
</ConceptCard>

<MythFact myth="There is no gravity in space, so astronauts float.">
At the height of the ISS (about 400 km) gravity is still about 89% as strong as on the ground. Astronauts float because they and the station are **falling together** around the Earth all the time: they are in free fall.
</MythFact>

<DidYouKnow>
Bhaskaracharya (Bhāskara II), writing in 12th-century India, described the Earth as having a natural power of attraction that pulls heavy objects towards it, centuries before Newton turned gravity into a precise law.
</DidYouKnow>
