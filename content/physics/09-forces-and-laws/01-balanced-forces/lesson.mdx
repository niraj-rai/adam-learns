<Stage kind="wonder" title="Why do things stop?" />

<Hook emoji="🛹">
Push a skateboard and let go: it rolls, slows and stops. For almost 2000 years people believed, like Aristotle, that moving things *naturally* stop, so a force is needed to keep anything moving. Galileo and Newton showed this is wrong. The skateboard stops because a force (friction) stops it.
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="A hockey puck slides across perfectly smooth ice with no friction and no air. What happens?"
  options={["It slows down and stops", "It speeds up", "It keeps moving at the same speed in a straight line", "It curves to one side"]}
  answer={2}
>
With **no resultant force**, nothing changes its motion. It glides on forever at the same velocity. That's Newton's first law.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Resultant force" />

<ConceptCard title="Adding forces along a line" emoji="➕">
Forces in the same direction add; forces in opposite directions subtract. The single force that has the same effect as all of them together is the <KeyTerm def="The overall force on an object when all the forces acting on it are combined, taking directions into account.">resultant force</KeyTerm>.
- 50 N right and 30 N left gives a resultant of **20 N right**.
- 40 N right and 40 N left gives a resultant of **0 N**: the forces are **balanced**.
</ConceptCard>

<Compare
  columns={[
    { title: "Balanced (resultant = 0)", emoji: "⚖️", points: ["At rest: stays at rest", "Moving: keeps the same speed and direction", "e.g. a car cruising at a steady 60 km/h: driving force = friction + air resistance"] },
    { title: "Unbalanced (resultant ≠ 0)", emoji: "↗️", points: ["Speeds up, slows down or changes direction", "The object accelerates in the direction of the resultant", "e.g. a bus pulling away from a stop"] }
  ]}
/>

<ConceptCard title="Newton's first law" emoji="🍎">
An object stays at rest, or keeps moving at a constant velocity, **unless a resultant force acts on it**. This tendency to resist changes in motion is called <KeyTerm def="The tendency of an object to keep doing what it is doing: to stay at rest or keep moving at the same velocity. More mass means more inertia.">inertia</KeyTerm>. The more mass something has, the more inertia it has: a loaded truck is much harder to start or stop than a bicycle.
</ConceptCard>

<Stage kind="try" title="Force Arena" />

<Lab id="force-arena" />

<MythFact myth="You need a force to keep something moving.">
You only need a force to **overcome friction and air resistance**. In space, where there is no friction, spacecraft like Voyager 1 have coasted for nearly 50 years with their engines off.
</MythFact>

<RealLife>
Seat belts exist because of inertia. When a car stops suddenly, your body keeps moving forwards at the car's old speed. The belt provides the force that stops you safely. Shake a tree and fruit falls: the branch moves, but the fruit's inertia keeps it where it was, and it breaks off.
</RealLife>
