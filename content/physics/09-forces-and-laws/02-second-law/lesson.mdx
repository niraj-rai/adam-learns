<Stage kind="wonder" title="Why is an empty trolley easier to push?" />

<Hook emoji="🛒">
Push an empty supermarket trolley and it shoots forward. Push a full one just as hard and it creeps. Push the full one harder and it speeds up faster. Newton turned these everyday experiences into one of the most useful equations in physics.
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="You double the resultant force on a trolley (same mass). What happens to its acceleration?"
  options={["It stays the same", "It doubles", "It halves", "It becomes four times bigger"]}
  answer={1}
>
Acceleration is **proportional** to the resultant force: double the force, double the acceleration.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Force, mass and acceleration" />

<Formula tex="F = m \times a" caption="F = resultant force (N), m = mass (kg), a = acceleration (m/s²)" />

<ConceptCard title="What the equation says" emoji="🧮">
- **a ∝ F**: for the same mass, a bigger resultant force gives a bigger acceleration.
- **a ∝ 1/m**: for the same force, a bigger mass gives a smaller acceleration.
- **1 newton** is the force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s². So 1 N = 1 kg m/s².
</ConceptCard>

<ConceptCard title="Weight is a force" emoji="🌍">
Near Earth's surface, everything falls with an acceleration of about **g = 9.8 m/s²** (ignoring air). Using F = ma, the force of gravity on a mass m is its <KeyTerm def="The force of gravity on an object, measured in newtons. W = mg.">weight</KeyTerm>:
</ConceptCard>

<Formula tex="W = m \times g" caption="On Earth g ≈ 9.8 N/kg, so a 50 kg student weighs about 490 N" />

<MythFact myth="A bigger force means a bigger speed.">
A force changes speed: it causes **acceleration**. A small force acting for a long time can build up a huge speed. Ion engines on spacecraft push with less force than the weight of a sheet of paper, yet after months they reach very high speeds.
</MythFact>
