<PredictFirst
  question="A 40 kg student travels to the Moon. What is their MASS there?"
  options={["40 kg", "About 6.6 kg", "0 kg", "240 kg"]}
  answer={0}
>
**Mass** is the amount of matter in you. You didn't leave any of yourself behind, so it's still **40 kg**! What changes is your **weight**: the pull of gravity on you.
</PredictFirst>

<Formula tex="\text{weight (N)} = \text{mass (kg)} \times g \ (\text{N/kg})" caption="On Earth, g ≈ 9.8 N/kg: every kilogram is pulled with about 9.8 N" />

<StepReveal>
<div>
**Worked example:** What does a 50 kg student weigh on Earth and on the Moon?
</div>
<div>
Earth: W = 50 × 9.8 = **490 N**
</div>
<div>
Moon: W = 50 × 1.6 = **80 N**, about one-sixth. Same mass, much smaller weight.
</div>
</StepReveal>

<ConceptCard title="Do heavy things fall faster?" emoji="🪶">
Without air, **no**: everything speeds up at the same rate. On Earth, a feather falls slowly only because **air resistance** pushes up on it a lot compared to its tiny weight. In 1971, astronaut David Scott dropped a hammer and a feather on the airless Moon, and they landed together.
</ConceptCard>

<MythFact myth="There's no gravity in space. That's why astronauts float.">
Gravity is still strong at the space station (about 90% of what it is on the ground!). Astronauts float because they and the station are **falling together** around the Earth. This is called free fall.
</MythFact>

<RealLife title="Chandrayaan-3 on the Moon">
In August 2023, ISRO's **Vikram lander** touched down near the Moon's south pole. With no air for parachutes, it used engines firing downward to slow its fall. The Moon's weaker gravity meant less thrust was needed.
</RealLife>

<CbseCorner source="Class 8 Curiosity Ch 5">
- **Gravitational force** is a non-contact force of attraction; Earth pulls all objects towards its centre.
- **Weight** is the force with which Earth pulls an object; measured in **newtons** with a spring balance.
- Mass is constant; weight on the Moon is about **1/6** of that on Earth.
</CbseCorner>
