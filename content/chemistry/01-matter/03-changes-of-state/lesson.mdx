<Stage kind="explain" title="Six changes of state" />

| Change | From → To | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Melting** | solid → liquid | ice cream melting in your hand |
| **Freezing** | liquid → solid | making ice in the freezer |
| **Evaporation / boiling** | liquid → gas | a puddle drying up; water boiling for chai |
| **Condensation** | gas → liquid | droplets on a cold glass of nimbu pani |
| **Sublimation** | solid → gas | camphor (kapur) disappearing from the puja thali |
| **Deposition** | gas → solid | frost forming on a cold window |

<ConceptCard title="Why the temperature stops rising" emoji="📈">
During melting and boiling, the heat energy is **not** used to make the particles move faster, so the temperature stays the same. Instead, the energy is used to **overcome the forces of attraction** between particles so they can break free into the next state.

This "hidden" energy is called <KeyTerm def="Energy absorbed or released when a substance changes state, without any change in temperature. 'Latent' means hidden.">latent heat</KeyTerm>.

- The **melting point** is the temperature at which a solid melts. It is the same as the freezing point.
- The **boiling point** is the temperature at which a liquid boils.
</ConceptCard>

<StepReveal>
<div>
**Evaporation, step by step.** Particles in a liquid move at different speeds. Some are much faster than average.
</div>
<div>
A fast particle **at the surface** can have enough energy to break away from its neighbours and escape into the air as a gas.
</div>
<div>
The fastest particles leave, so the **average energy of those left behind drops**. The liquid cools down. This is why sweating cools you, and why water stays cool in a clay **matka**!
</div>
<div>
Evaporation is faster with **higher temperature**, **larger surface area**, **wind**, and **lower humidity** (drier air).
</div>
</StepReveal>

<MythFact myth="The bubbles in boiling water are air bubbles.">
The big bubbles in boiling water are **water vapour**: pockets of water in the gas state forming inside the liquid. (The tiny bubbles you see *before* boiling are dissolved air coming out.)
</MythFact>

<MythFact myth="The white cloud coming out of a pressure cooker is steam.">
Real steam (water vapour) is **invisible**. Look closely at the gap just above the whistle: it's clear! The white cloud is made of tiny droplets of **liquid** water that form when the steam condenses in the cooler air.
</MythFact>

<Formula tex="T(\mathrm{K}) = T(^\circ\mathrm{C}) + 273" caption="Scientists often use the kelvin (K) scale. 0 °C = 273 K, and 100 °C = 373 K." />

<QuickCheck question="What is the boiling point of water in kelvin?" options={["100 K", "173 K", "273 K", "373 K"]} answer={3}>
100 + 273 = **373 K**. On the kelvin scale, there are no negative temperatures. 0 K is the coldest possible temperature!
</QuickCheck>

<RealLife title="The clay matka">
Water in an earthen pot (**matka**) stays cool on hot days. The clay has tiny pores, so water seeps to the outer surface and **evaporates**, taking energy away from the water inside. It is a fridge with no electricity, used in India for thousands of years.
</RealLife>
