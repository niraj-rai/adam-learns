<Hook emoji="🔮">
In 1869, the Russian chemist **Dmitri Mendeleev** arranged the 63 known elements into a table and left **gaps**. He boldly predicted the properties of elements nobody had found yet. Within 20 years, three of them were discovered, and they matched his predictions almost exactly!
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="How are elements ordered in the modern periodic table?"
  options={["Alphabetically", "By the year they were discovered", "By atomic number (number of protons)", "By colour"]}
  answer={2}
>
By **atomic number**. Hydrogen (1) comes first, then helium (2), lithium (3) and so on. Mendeleev used atomic mass, but after protons were discovered, scientists switched to atomic number, which fixed a few odd placements.
</PredictFirst>

<ConceptCard title="Where are the metals?" emoji="🧭">
- **Metals** are on the **left** and in the middle. They make up about three-quarters of all elements.
- **Non-metals** are on the **right** (plus hydrogen, which is a special case at the top left).
- A zig-zag staircase of **metalloids** (like boron and silicon) separates them. They have properties in between.
</ConceptCard>

<MythFact myth="Mendeleev's table was just a list.">
Mendeleev's genius was trusting the **pattern** more than the data. When an element didn't fit, he left a gap or suggested its measured mass was wrong, and he was often right! A good model doesn't just describe; it **predicts**.
</MythFact>

<DidYouKnow>
Today there are **118** known elements. The newest ones (up to oganesson, 118) were made in laboratories and last only fractions of a second. Element 101 is named **mendelevium** in Mendeleev's honour.
</DidYouKnow>
