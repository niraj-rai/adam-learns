<Stage kind="wonder" title="Drinking water from the sea?" />

<Hook emoji="🚢">
Sailors on a ship are surrounded by water, but sea water is too salty to drink. Evaporation would give them salt and lose the water. Is there a way to keep the **water** instead?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="You boil salt water and cool the steam on a cold plate. The drops that form will be…"
  options={["Salty", "Pure water, not salty", "Pure salt", "Half salt, half water"]}
  answer={1}
>
Only the **water** turns into vapour at 100 °C. The salt stays behind in the pan. When the steam cools and condenses, you get **pure water**. That's **distillation**!
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Distillation: evaporate, then condense" />

<ConceptCard title="Simple distillation" emoji="⚗️">
Distillation separates a **solvent** from a **solution** and **keeps both**.

1. The solution is heated in a flask. The solvent **boils** and turns to vapour.
2. The vapour passes into a **condenser**, a tube surrounded by a jacket of cold flowing water.
3. The vapour **condenses** back into a liquid, the **distillate**, which drips into a beaker.
4. The dissolved solid stays in the flask.

It combines two changes of state you already know: **evaporation/boiling** and **condensation**.
</ConceptCard>

<Lab id="distillation" />

<ConceptCard title="Fractional distillation" emoji="🏭">
What if you need to separate two **liquids** that mix completely, like alcohol and water? They have different boiling points (78 °C and 100 °C).

A **fractionating column** is added above the flask. The liquid with the **lower boiling point** reaches the top first and is collected first.

It is used on a huge scale to separate **crude oil** into petrol, diesel and kerosene, and to separate **liquid air** into oxygen and nitrogen for hospitals.
</ConceptCard>

<Stage kind="try" title="Chromatography: solve the ink mystery" />

<Lab id="chromatography" />

<ConceptCard title="How chromatography works" emoji="🌈">
A spot of the mixture goes on a **pencil line** near the bottom of the paper. A solvent (like water) soaks up the paper and carries the substances with it.

Substances that dissolve **better** in the solvent, and stick **less** to the paper, travel **further**. So different dyes end up at different heights.
</ConceptCard>

<Formula tex="R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by the substance}}{\text{distance moved by the solvent}}" caption="Rf is always between 0 and 1. The same substance in the same solvent always has the same Rf, like a fingerprint." />

<MythFact myth="Black ink is just one black substance.">
Most black inks are **mixtures** of several coloured dyes: often blue, red and yellow. Chromatography reveals the hidden colours. Forensic scientists use this to match ink from documents, and food scientists use it to check for illegal food colours.
</MythFact>

<QuickCheck question="Which method would you use to separate kerosene from water? (They don't mix, and kerosene floats.)" options={["Distillation", "Separating funnel", "Chromatography", "Centrifugation"]} answer={1}>
Kerosene and water are **immiscible**: they form two layers. Open the tap of a **separating funnel** to drain off the bottom layer (water), then collect the kerosene.

<RealLife title="Water for Chennai from the sea">
Chennai runs large **desalination** plants that turn sea water into drinking water for lakhs of people. Most use **reverse osmosis**: sea water is pushed at high pressure through a special membrane that lets water through but blocks salt. It is like a super-fine filter, and it needs a lot of energy.
</RealLife>

- Know which method separates which mixture: **distillation** (acetone + water, boiling points far apart), **fractional distillation** (liquids with close boiling points; gases from air), **chromatography** (dyes in ink), **centrifugation** (cream from milk), **separating funnel** (oil + water), **sublimation** (salt + camphor or ammonium chloride), **crystallisation** (pure copper sulfate, alum).
- Be able to **label the distillation apparatus**: flask, thermometer, condenser (water in at the bottom, out at the top), receiver.
