<PredictFirst
  question="You weigh a glass of water (200 g) and some sugar (20 g). You dissolve the sugar. What does the sugary water weigh now?"
  options={["200 g, because the sugar disappeared", "Less than 200 g", "220 g", "More than 220 g"]}
  answer={2}
>
It weighs **220 g**. The sugar hasn't disappeared: its particles have spread out between the water particles, too small to see. **Mass is conserved** when things dissolve.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="The language of solutions" />

<ConceptCard title="Solute + solvent = solution" emoji="🧪">
- The **solute** is the substance that dissolves (sugar).
- The **solvent** is the substance it dissolves in (water).
- The **solution** is the mixture formed (sugar water). It is **homogeneous**.

A substance that dissolves is **soluble** (salt, sugar). One that doesn't is **insoluble** (sand, chalk). Water dissolves so many things that it's called the **universal solvent**.
</ConceptCard>

<MythFact myth="Dissolving and melting are the same thing.">
**Melting** needs only one substance and heat: solid ice turns into liquid water. **Dissolving** needs **two** substances: the solute's particles spread out between the solvent's particles. Sugar dissolves in water at room temperature. It doesn't melt until about 186 °C!
</MythFact>

<Stage kind="try" title="Dissolve until you can't" />

Pick potassium nitrate, add it a little at a time, and find the point where no more will dissolve. Then record your data at different temperatures to build a **solubility curve**.

<Lab id="dissolving-lab" solute="kno3" />

<ConceptCard title="Saturated solutions and solubility" emoji="📈">
A **saturated** solution holds the **maximum** amount of solute that can dissolve at that temperature. Add more and it just sits at the bottom. A solution that could dissolve more is **unsaturated**.

**Solubility** is the maximum mass of solute that dissolves in **100 g of solvent** at a given temperature. For potassium nitrate at 20 °C, that's about **32 g per 100 g of water**.

A **solubility curve** shows how solubility changes with temperature. For most solids, it goes **up** as the temperature rises, but not all by the same amount: salt barely changes!
</ConceptCard>

<QuickCheck question="Using the textbook curve, about how much potassium nitrate dissolves in 100 g of water at 60 °C?" options={["About 11 g", "About 37 g", "About 110 g", "About 246 g"]} answer={2}>
About **110 g**, more than the mass of the water itself! Salt only manages about 37 g at the same temperature.
</QuickCheck>

<StepReveal>
<div>
**What makes a solid dissolve FASTER?** (It changes how fast it dissolves, not how much.)
</div>
<div>
🔥 **Heating:** the particles move faster, so they collide and spread more quickly.
</div>
<div>
🥄 **Stirring:** it carries dissolved particles away and brings fresh solvent to the solute.
</div>
<div>
🔨 **Crushing into smaller pieces:** there is more surface area for the solvent to attack. That's why powdered sugar dissolves faster than a sugar cube.
</div>
</StepReveal>

<ConceptCard title="Concentration" emoji="🧮">
The **concentration** tells you how much solute is dissolved in a certain amount of solution.
</ConceptCard>

<Formula tex="\text{concentration (\%)} = \frac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 100" caption="Mass of solution = mass of solute + mass of solvent. Example: 20 g sugar in 180 g water → 20 ÷ 200 × 100 = 10%" />

<DidYouKnow>
Gases dissolve too, but **less** in warm water. That's why a cold soda stays fizzy longer, and why fish in warm, polluted lakes can struggle to get enough dissolved oxygen.
</DidYouKnow>
