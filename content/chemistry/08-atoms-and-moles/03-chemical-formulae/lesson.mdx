<Stage kind="wonder" title="Why is it Al₂O₃?" />

<Hook emoji="✖️">
The formula of rust-like aluminium oxide is Al₂O₃, not AlO. Chemists don't memorise thousands of formulae: they work them out from **valencies**, making sure the positive and negative charges cancel exactly.
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="Calcium ions are Ca²⁺ and chloride ions are Cl⁻. What is the formula of calcium chloride?"
  options={["CaCl", "Ca₂Cl", "CaCl₂", "Ca₂Cl₂"]}
  answer={2}
>
One Ca²⁺ (+2) needs **two** Cl⁻ (−1 each) to balance: **CaCl₂**.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="The criss-cross method" />

<StepReveal
  steps={[
    "Write the symbols: positive ion (metal) first, then the negative ion.",
    "Write each valency above its symbol.",
    "Cross them over: each valency becomes the number of the OTHER ion.",
    "Divide by any common factor to get the simplest ratio (Ca₂O₂ → CaO).",
    "Put a polyatomic ion in brackets if you need more than one: Ca(OH)₂.",
    "Check: total positive charge = total negative charge."
  ]}
/>

<ConceptCard title="Polyatomic ions" emoji="🧱">
Some ions are groups of atoms with an overall charge: <KeyTerm def="An ion made of two or more atoms bonded together, with an overall charge, e.g. SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺.">polyatomic ions</KeyTerm>. Common ones: hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, sulfate SO₄²⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, phosphate PO₄³⁻ and ammonium NH₄⁺.
</ConceptCard>

<MythFact myth="You can change the small numbers in a formula to balance an equation.">
The small numbers (subscripts) are fixed by the valencies: changing them makes a different substance. To balance an equation you only change the big numbers in front of formulae.
</MythFact>
