<Hook emoji="🧺">
A shopkeeper doesn't count every grain of rice: they weigh it. If you know how much one grain weighs, the mass tells you how many there are. Chemists do the same with atoms, using a special counting unit called the **mole**.
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="Which contains more atoms: 12 g of carbon or 12 g of magnesium? (C = 12, Mg = 24)"
  options={["12 g of magnesium", "12 g of carbon", "They contain the same number", "Impossible to tell"]}
  answer={1}
>
12 g of carbon is **1 mole** of atoms; 12 g of magnesium is only **0.5 mole**, because each magnesium atom is twice as heavy.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="The mole" />

<ConceptCard title="What is a mole?" emoji="🧺">
A <KeyTerm def="The amount of substance containing 6.022 × 10²³ particles (atoms, molecules or ions). Symbol mol.">mole</KeyTerm> is 6.022 × 10²³ particles, just as a dozen is 12. This number is the <KeyTerm def="The number of particles in one mole: 6.022 × 10²³.">Avogadro constant</KeyTerm> (Nᴀ).

The mass of one mole, the <KeyTerm def="The mass of one mole of a substance, in g/mol. Numerically equal to its atomic or molecular mass.">molar mass</KeyTerm>, equals the atomic or molecular mass in grams: 1 mol of C = 12 g, 1 mol of H₂O = 18 g.
</ConceptCard>

<Formula tex="n = \frac{m}{M} \qquad N = n \times N_A" caption="n = moles, m = mass (g), M = molar mass (g/mol), N = number of particles" />

<DidYouKnow>
The number is named after Amedeo Avogadro, an Italian scientist who suggested in 1811 that equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of particles. He never knew the actual number: it was measured nearly 100 years later.
</DidYouKnow>
