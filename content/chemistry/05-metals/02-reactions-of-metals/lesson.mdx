In a school lab, **sodium** is stored in a bottle of **kerosene**. It is soft enough to cut with a butter knife. But drop a tiny piece into water and it fizzes, races around and can even burst into flame! Meanwhile, **gold** coins found in ancient ruins still shine. Why are metals so different?

<PredictFirst

Sodium is so **reactive** that it reacts with oxygen and water vapour in the air, and can catch fire. Kerosene keeps the air away. Potassium is stored the same way.

<MythFact myth="All metals react with water.">
Only the **most reactive** metals (potassium, sodium, calcium) react quickly with cold water. Magnesium reacts slowly (faster with steam). Iron only rusts slowly over days, and copper, silver and gold don't react with water at all. That's why we can make water pipes and pots from some metals!
</MythFact>

<DidYouKnow>
Burning **magnesium** gives out such a brilliant white light that it was used in early camera flashes. It's still used in fireworks and emergency flares. Never look directly at it: it's bright enough to hurt your eyes!
</DidYouKnow>
