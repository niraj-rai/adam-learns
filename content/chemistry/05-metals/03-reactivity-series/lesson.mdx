<Hook emoji="🪢">
Drop an **iron nail** into blue **copper sulfate** solution. After a while, the nail turns reddish-brown and the blue colour fades. But put a **copper** wire into **iron sulfate** solution and nothing happens at all. Why does it only work one way round?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="Why does iron push copper out of copper sulfate, but copper can't push iron out of iron sulfate?"
  options={["Iron is heavier", "Iron is more reactive than copper", "Copper is magnetic", "Blue solutions are special"]}
  answer={1}
>
Iron is **more reactive** than copper, so it "wins the tug-of-war" and takes copper's place in the compound. Copper, being less reactive, can't displace iron.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="The reactivity series" />

<ConceptCard title="Most reactive to least reactive" emoji="📊">
Chemists list metals in order of reactivity:

**Potassium > Sodium > Calcium > Magnesium > Aluminium > Zinc > Iron > Lead > (Hydrogen) > Copper > Mercury > Silver > Gold**

A handy memory aid: *"**P**lease **S**end **C**ats **M**onkeys **A**nd **Z**ebras **I**n **L**arge **H**ired **C**ages **M**ade of **S**ilver and **G**old."*

Metals above **hydrogen** react with dilute acids to give hydrogen; metals below it don't.
</ConceptCard>

<ConceptCard title="Displacement reactions" emoji="🔁">
A **more reactive** metal **displaces** (pushes out) a **less reactive** metal from a solution of its compound:

iron + copper sulfate → iron sulfate + copper

If the metal is **less reactive**, there is **no reaction**.
</ConceptCard>

<MythFact myth="In displacement, the metal just disappears.">
Nothing disappears (conservation of mass!). The more reactive metal **turns into a salt** that dissolves in the solution, while the less reactive metal **comes out as a solid** coating. The atoms have simply swapped partners.
</MythFact>

<DidYouKnow>
The reactivity series explains the history of metals! Unreactive **gold** and **copper** are found almost pure in nature, so they were used thousands of years ago. Very reactive **aluminium** is locked tightly in rocks. It was once more precious than gold and was only produced cheaply after 1886, using electricity.
</DidYouKnow>
