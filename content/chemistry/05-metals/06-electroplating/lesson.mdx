<Hook emoji="🚿">
Bathroom taps are shiny silver, but underneath they're made of brass. Many "gold" medals are mostly other metals with just a thin layer of gold on top. How do you wrap an object in a metal layer thinner than a hair?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="You dip the two wires of a battery-and-bulb tester into DISTILLED water. What happens?"
  options={["The bulb glows brightly", "The bulb barely glows or stays off", "The water boils", "The bulb explodes"]}
  answer={1}
>
Pure (distilled) water is a **very poor conductor**. It has almost no **ions** (charged particles) to carry the current. But add a pinch of salt and the bulb lights up! Tap water conducts a little because of dissolved salts, which is why electricity near water is so dangerous.
</PredictFirst>

<Stage kind="explain" title="Liquids that conduct" />

<ConceptCard title="Ions carry the current" emoji="⚡">
Liquids conduct electricity when they contain **ions**: atoms or groups of atoms with an electric charge. Salts, acids and bases form ions when they dissolve in water.

A liquid that conducts and is changed by the current is called an **electrolyte**. The metal rods dipped into it are **electrodes**.
</ConceptCard>

<Stage kind="try" title="Test liquids, then electroplate" />

<Lab id="electroplating" />

<StepReveal>
<div>
**How electroplating works:** the object to be plated (an iron key) is connected to the **negative** terminal. This is the **cathode**.
</div>
<div>
A plate of the coating metal (copper) is connected to the **positive** terminal. This is the **anode**.
</div>
<div>
Both dip into a solution of a salt of the coating metal (copper sulfate). The **copper ions** are positively charged, so they are attracted to the negative key.
</div>
<div>
At the key, the ions become **copper atoms** and form a thin, even coating. Meanwhile, the copper plate slowly dissolves to replace the ions, so the solution stays blue.
</div>
</StepReveal>

<MythFact myth="Electroplating is like painting metal on.">
Paint is a separate layer that can peel off. Electroplating builds up a layer of **metal atoms, one by one**, that bonds to the surface. That's why chrome-plated handlebars and gold-plated jewellery stay shiny for years.
</MythFact>

<RealLife title="Electroplating everywhere">
- **Chromium** plating on bicycle handlebars and car parts: shiny and scratch-resistant.
- **Tin** plating on steel food cans: tin is less reactive, so it doesn't react with food.
- **Zinc** plating (galvanising) on iron: protects against rust.
- **Silver and gold** plating on cheaper jewellery and medals.
</RealLife>
