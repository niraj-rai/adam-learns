<Stage kind="wonder" title="Melt it or cook it?" />

<Hook emoji="🧈">
Heat butter in a pan and it melts. Keep heating and it turns brown and smells nutty. Both happened because of heat, so are they the same kind of change?
</Hook>

<PredictFirst
  question="Which of these makes a NEW substance?"
  options={["Melting butter", "Browning butter until it smells nutty", "Cutting butter into cubes", "Putting butter in the fridge"]}
  answer={1}
>
Browning makes **new substances** with a new colour and smell, and you can't turn it back into plain butter. That is a **chemical change**. Melting, cutting and chilling only change the state or shape: **physical changes**.
</PredictFirst>

<Compare

<ConceptCard title="In the particle model" emoji="⚛️">
In a **physical** change, the particles themselves stay the same. Water molecules are still water molecules, whether they are in ice, liquid or steam.

In a **chemical** change, atoms **break apart and join up in new ways**, making brand-new particles. That's why the new substance has **new properties**. A chemical change is also called a **chemical reaction**.
</ConceptCard>

<Stage kind="try" title="Be the Change Detective" />

<Lab id="change-detective" />

<MythFact myth="If something was heated, it must be a chemical change.">
Heating can cause **either** kind. Heating ice makes it melt: physical. Heating sugar until it turns into brown caramel: chemical. Always ask: **was a new substance made?**
</MythFact>

<MythFact myth="Every physical change is easy to reverse.">
Breaking a glass or cutting your hair are physical changes (still glass, still hair), but you can't easily reverse them! Reversibility is a **clue**, not the rule. The real test is whether a **new substance** formed.
</MythFact>

<DidYouKnow>
When you cook food, many chemical changes happen. The golden-brown colour and amazing smell of a crispy dosa, toasted bread or roasted peanuts come from the **Maillard reaction**, a chemical reaction between sugars and proteins at high temperature.
</DidYouKnow>
